Activity 2 — Task 2
Data Visualizations

The following plots were chosen to best represent the distribution and relationships within the IPL dataset. Each visualization includes the reasoning for selection and interpretation of trends observed.

Bar Chart
Total Runs by Batting Team
📌 Why this plot? A bar chart is ideal for comparing a numerical value (total runs) across categorical groups (teams). It makes cross-team comparison straightforward and intuitive.
Interpretation: Teams like Mumbai Indians and Chennai Super Kings consistently score higher total runs, reflecting their strong batting line-ups and frequent finals appearances across IPL seasons.
Pie / Donut Chart
Distribution of Dismissal Types
📌 Why this plot? A pie chart effectively shows part-to-whole relationships. Since dismissal_kind is a nominal categorical variable with a finite set of types, it shows the proportion of each dismissal mode.
Interpretation: "Caught" is the most common mode of dismissal, accounting for over 50% of wickets, followed by "bowled." Run-outs and stumpings are comparatively rare.
Line Chart
Average Runs per Over (Run Rate Progression)
📌 Why this plot? A line chart is best suited for showing trends over a sequential variable. Since "over" is an ordered interval variable, plotting average runs per over reveals scoring patterns across a match.
Interpretation: Run rates are highest in the powerplay (overs 1–6) and the death overs (17–20), with a dip in the middle overs.
Histogram
Distribution of Batsman Runs per Delivery
📌 Why this plot? A histogram is appropriate for a discrete numerical variable like batsman_runs to show its frequency distribution.
Interpretation: The distribution is heavily right-skewed — dot balls (0 runs) are most frequent, followed by singles. Boundaries (4s and 6s) occur far less often.

Extra Activity 5 · Week 6
Discrete Random Variable Modelling

The IPL Ball-by-Ball dataset was modelled using a Poisson Distribution to represent the number of events (e.g. wickets, boundaries, extras) occurring per over.

Why Poisson Distribution?

The Poisson distribution models the number of times an event occurs in a fixed interval given that events occur independently at a constant average rate (λ). In cricket, occurrences like wickets per over satisfy these conditions — making Poisson an appropriate model to fit and verify against real IPL data.

P(X = k) = (λᵏ · e⁻λ) / k!   |   where λ = average rate, k = number of events
1

Data Collection & Variable Selection

The variable wickets per over was selected. Data was aggregated per over across all matches to get the observed frequency distribution of wicket counts per over.

2

Estimating λ (Mean Rate)

The Poisson parameter λ was estimated as the sample mean of wickets per over from the observed data, used to compute expected frequencies.

3

Computing Expected Frequencies

Using the Poisson formula, expected probabilities were calculated for each k and multiplied by total observations to get expected frequencies.

4

Verification & Interpretation

Observed vs expected frequencies were tabulated and plotted. The model was verified by checking that mean ≈ variance, a key property of the Poisson distribution.
